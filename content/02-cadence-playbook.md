# The 90-day marketing cadence for a startup with product-market fit

A content cadence is what your marketing actually looks like: not the strategy doc, but the rhythm of work that produces the pipeline.

Most startups don't have one. They have a plan for launch week and hope for the rest.

This is a 90-day cadence you can run yourself. It assumes one thing you should check first: product-market fit.

If you don't have it yet, skip marketing for now and fix the product. Marketing accelerates fit; it doesn't create it.

## Weeks 1–2: the channel plan and audit-lite

The first two weeks are planning, not producing. You're deciding what to ship and where, and you're finding out what you already have.

### The audit-lite

An audit-lite is a two-hour pass over what exists, not a deep review. Pick two channels, not eight, because wide mixes are hard to sustain.

Look at what you've published in the last six months and what came of it, then repurpose what worked and kill what didn't.

Check your analytics basics. Where do the visitors you already have come from, and which few pages earn the attention?

Write down three things: your best content asset, your best channel, and the one gap that matters. That's the whole audit.

### The channel plan

Choose two or three channels, and give each a job: one pulls new attention, one builds trust, and one captures buyers who are ready to move.

A B2B software company might run a search-optimized blog and a weekly newsletter. A consumer brand might run short video and email.

Set the cadence numbers. The starting point for an early team is one shippable a week on your main channel, and one distribution touch per shippable.

Put the numbers on a calendar you can see. The calendar is the contract, and it's the thing that keeps the engine honest.

Plan the first month of pieces now. Four topics, scheduled on dates, on the calendar before week one ends.

## The weekly shippable

From week three on, the unit of work is the weekly shippable. One piece of content ships on schedule, and it gets distributed.

Ship is the operative word: done means published, not drafted, and a piece that waits for polish doesn't exist.

Pick a ship day and defend it. The marketing calendar blocks that slot in your week the same way you'd block a product sprint.

The shape of a shippable is simple. A topic from the plan, written in a day or two, published on schedule, and pushed to the audience.

Distribution is not optional; a shippable without a distribution touch is a diary entry. One shared post, one newsletter slot, or one intro to a conversation counts.

Schedule distribution at the same time you schedule the piece. If distribution is an afterthought, it won't happen.

Set a time budget and protect it. A realistic early-team budget is four to six hours a week for production and one for distribution.

If a week runs over, ship the piece anyway. Shipped and imperfect beats perfect and absent, every time.

Keep the publish log current: one row per week, with date, piece, and channel. It's the evidence the cadence exists.

## What 90 days looks like

Month one is the setup month: the channel plan is written, the calendar is full, and the first four shippables go out on schedule. Expect the numbers to be flat, because they usually are in month one.

Month two is the rhythm month, when the ship day stops feeling novel and starts feeling normal. You'll see the first useful signal in the readout: which topic got a reply, which post pulled a sign-up.

Month three is the decision month. With roughly a dozen shippables and two readouts behind you, you can say which channel earns the pipeline and which one you're cutting.

The honest expectation is modest. Three months of one shippable a week won't flood you with leads, and no one honest will promise that it will. What it buys you is structure: a rhythm, a log, and a decision about where to put the next three months.

That decision is the real output of the 90 days. It's worth far more than whatever the twelve pieces earned on their own.

## The monthly readout

Once a month, stop producing and measure. This is the readout, and it's the part most founders skip.

### What moved

Collect the numbers tied to the pipeline, not the vanity counts: leads, sign-ups, replies, sales conversations — and watch traffic and rankings only as context.

One month is a small sample. You're looking for direction, not proof, and you should say that to yourself plainly at month one.

### What didn't

Name the piece that flopped and the channel that stalled. Point the fix at an answer: the topic missed, the channel is wrong, or the distribution was absent.

### What we change next

The readout ends with exactly one change. One change keeps focus; two dilute it; three mean you weren't measuring.

Change the mix, change the cadence, or change the topic. Then run another month and read again.

Watch for the honest signals. If nothing moved and you still can't name why, the channel plan is the problem, not the content.

The readout works because it forces a decision. A monthly decision is how the engine learns, and it's how you keep the pipeline from tapering.

## What is not the work

The cadence is a container, and it's worth saying plainly what doesn't belong in it.

Channel-hopping is not the work. A new platform account every month means no channel gets real attention, and nothing compounds.

Vanity metrics are not the work. Follower counts and impressions feel productive and tell you nothing about the pipeline.

Posting without a plan is not the work. Content without a channel job is noise, and noise doesn't move buyers.

Perfection is not the work. A perfect piece you never publish teaches you nothing.

Strategy that never ships is not the work. The strategy is the start, and the cadence is the work.

## Running this cadence with a team

The cadence scales exactly as written. The difference is who produces the shippables.

On your own, the founder is the producer, and the founder is the bottleneck the cadence was built to identify.

With a fractional hire, the strategy gets owned and the production still waits for someone with hours. It's a common dead end, and it survives in the form of monthly strategy meetings with an empty calendar.

With an in-house hire, you trade hiring risk and payroll for control of a channel you might not understand yet.

With an execution team, the plan, the production, and the measurement all have an owner. You keep the review; you don't keep the production.

Every option moves the same three numbers: shippables per week, distribution touchpoints, and readout changes. Whoever owns all three owns the throughput.
