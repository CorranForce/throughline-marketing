# In-house team, agency, or an execution partner: how to decide

At some point, every startup founder who has shipped a product looks up and admits the marketing isn't happening.

Then the debate starts: hire in-house, hire an agency, hire a fractional CMO, or do it all yourself.

The debate usually runs on vague fears: losing control, wasting money, getting locked in.

This is a framework for the actual decision. It's built on four real trade-offs, and it's written so you can decide more clearly — even if the answer isn't us.

## The four trade-offs

Every marketing option trades the same four things: cost, control, hiring risk, and throughput.

Cost is the easy one to see. It's salary, retainer, or hours.

Control is harder. It's who owns the message, the accounts, and the decisions.

Hiring risk is the hidden one. It's the cost of a hire who doesn't work out — the search, the ramp, the exit, and the months lost in between.

Throughput is the one almost nobody names. It's the gap between what the plan says and what actually ships, week after week.

Most founders choose on cost and control. That's how the throughput gap survives.

## The options, honestly

### Doing it yourself

Doing it yourself is cheapest on cash and most expensive on your calendar. You own the message and the decisions, and you are the production team.

The math only works while marketing is a side project. The moment it needs a weekly rhythm, you're the bottleneck, and the rhythm breaks the first week your calendar fills.

### Hiring in-house

An in-house hire makes sense when marketing is going to stay core to your business for years. You get control, a dedicated calendar, and someone who lives inside the product.

You also get the full cost. Salary, benefits, tools, and management time — plus the hiring risk.

The hiring risk deserves to be stated plainly. At one hire, your options are a junior who needs training or a senior who costs the enterprise rate — and both gambles usually take about three months to resolve.

Bringing on the first hire also creates a new job for you. You become a marketing manager, and managing is a job of its own that eats your hours.

In-house is right when you've decided marketing is core and you'll hire a team eventually. Start with one strong hire and a clear pipeline of work, and it compounds.

### Hiring an agency

An agency makes sense when you want external expertise on a defined scope — a launch, a campaign, or a channel you don't know how to run.

You buy a team instead of a person. The cost is a retainer, and the control is shared by contract.

Watch the two failure modes. Scope creep turns a fixed project into a subscription you half-use, and a brief that's thin on facts comes back as generic work because the agency can only execute what you give them.

Agencies vary wildly in who does the work, so ask who touches your account and how often. The answer should be the people in the room during the pitch.

### Hiring a fractional CMO

A fractional CMO gives you strategy without a full hire. You get a senior operator for a few days a month, which sounds ideal for an early company.

It often becomes a dead end, and the cause is structural. A fractional CMO produces a roadmap, and the roadmap still needs someone to execute it.

If your company has no one to execute, the roadmap lands on top of your founder to-do list, where the strategy already lived. You've paid for a plan you already had, and the gap between plan and ship remains exactly where it was.

A fractional CMO helps when you already have an execution engine and you need senior direction on top of it. It doesn't build the engine for you.

## The "you are the bottleneck" test

Here's the test that settles most of these debates. Answer honestly: what stopped the last three months of marketing?

If the answer is "I didn't have the hours," you have a throughput gap, and hiring another strategist won't close it. The strategy isn't the bottleneck; the production is.

If the answer is "I didn't know what to do," you have a strategy gap, and a fractional CMO or an agency brief can genuinely help.

Most founders answer the first way and hire for the second. That mismatch is the whole reason the marketing budget keeps disappearing.

Run the test on the options too: in-house gives you throughput at the cost of hiring risk, an agency gives you throughput on a defined scope, and a fractional CMO gives you strategy and adds no throughput on its own.

## The option the hire-first debate misses

The framework has a fourth answer, and it's worth naming because it fits the founders who fail the bottleneck test.

An execution partner is a small team that plans, produces, and measures on a monthly cadence. A channel mix matched to your buyers, a content schedule that ships, and a monthly readout that changes what you do next.

You keep the strategy call and the product decisions. The production gets an owner, and the pipeline stops depending on your calendar.

The honest caveats apply here too. An execution partner works only with real access — product, pricing, analytics — and a short weekly review loop on your side. It's also a relationship: pick a team you can talk to plainly.

## How to actually decide

Write your answers to these five questions, and the decision falls out of them.

First: is marketing core to your business in three years? If yes, in-house is a legitimate investment, and you should start the hiring process now.

Second: are you hiring because you have a strategy gap or a throughput gap? If it's throughput, a strategist isn't the answer.

Third: can you spend three months and the search cost on a single hire? If not, in-house is a roll of the dice you don't need.

Fourth: do you have the facts to brief someone well — target buyer, product, pricing, current analytics? Without those, no option works, and the first step is assembling them.

Fifth: who will own the calendar while this arrangement runs? If the answer is you, the arrangement isn't fixing the bottleneck, it's decorating it.
